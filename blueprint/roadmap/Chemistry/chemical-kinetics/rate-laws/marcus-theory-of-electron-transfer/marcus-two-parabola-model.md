# Marcus two-parabola model and the reorganization energy

In Marcus theory the free energies of the donor and acceptor states along a solvent coordinate $q$ are equal-curvature parabolas offset by $\Delta G^\circ$; the reorganization energy $\lambda$ is the acceptor-state energy at the donor's equilibrium configuration above its own minimum.

For electron transfer $\mathrm D+\mathrm A\to\mathrm D^++\mathrm A^-$ between weakly coupled diabatic states mediated by
a collective nuclear (solvent) coordinate $q$, the **two-parabola model** takes the free energies
(potentials of mean force) of the initial (donor) and final (acceptor) states to be

$$G_D(q)=\tfrac12m\omega_0^2(q-d_D)^2,\qquad G_A(q)=\tfrac12m\omega_0^2(q-d_A)^2+\Delta G^\circ,$$

with a common curvature $m\omega_0^2>0$, equilibrium positions $d_D\neq d_A$, and $\Delta G^\circ$ the standard
free energy change for transfer of the electron from donor to acceptor. The **reorganization energy** is

$$\lambda=G_A(d_D)-G_A(d_A)=\tfrac12m\omega_0^2(d_D-d_A)^2>0,$$

the free energy that would be dissipated on the acceptor surface if the electron jumped at the donor's
equilibrium configuration; it also equals $G_D(d_A)-G_D(d_D)$. $-\Delta G^\circ$ is called the driving force.

## Context

Graduate physical chemistry. The model underlies electron-transfer rates in solution, at electrodes and in electronically conducting molecular solids and frameworks.

## Expansion

A model definition; the equal-curvature (linear-response) assumption makes the two parabolas translates of each other, which is what gives the quadratic barrier law.
