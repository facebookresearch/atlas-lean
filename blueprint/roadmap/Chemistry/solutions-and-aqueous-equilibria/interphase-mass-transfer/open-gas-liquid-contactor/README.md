# External balance of an open gas–liquid contactor

In an open contactor, let the solute amounts $n_g$ and $n_l$ stored in the gas and in the liquid be
differentiable functions of time that obey the balances
$n_g' = G_{\mathrm{in}} - G_{\mathrm{out}} - \dot N$ and $n_l' = L_{\mathrm{in}} - L_{\mathrm{out}} + \dot N$,
where $G_{\mathrm{in}}, G_{\mathrm{out}}, L_{\mathrm{in}}, L_{\mathrm{out}}$ are the solute flow rates into and
out of each phase and $\dot N$ is the interphase solute transfer rate from gas to liquid. Then the total
stored solute obeys $(n_g + n_l)' = G_{\mathrm{in}} + L_{\mathrm{in}} - G_{\mathrm{out}} - L_{\mathrm{out}}$,
whatever $\dot N$ is. At steady state, where both accumulation rates vanish, the total solute flow
entering equals the total solute flow leaving.

## Idea

What the gas loses to the liquid, the liquid gains, so adding the two phase balances cancels the
transfer term: the total solute in the contactor changes only through its four external ports. The
balance therefore needs no model of the transfer, neither Henry's law nor a mass-transfer coefficient,
and at steady state it is the overall solute balance of an absorber or a stripper. The transfer it
leaves aside is what the enclosing topic, interphase mass transfer, describes per unit interfacial area
and per unit volume; here $\dot N$ is the total amount of solute that crosses the interface per unit
time. A closed vessel's relaxation towards equilibrium is a different result: it needs a constitutive
law for $\dot N$, which these balances do not give.
