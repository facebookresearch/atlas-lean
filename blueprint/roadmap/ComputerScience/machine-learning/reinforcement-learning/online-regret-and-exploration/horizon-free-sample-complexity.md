# Horizon-free sample complexity for stationary episodic MDPs

In an episodic MDP with time-invariant transitions and rewards, nonnegative rewards, total reward at most $1$ per episode and a horizon $H$ of order at least $(S+1)\log(S+1)$, an $\varepsilon$-optimal non-stationary policy can be found with probability at least $1-\delta$ from $(S+1)^{O(S)}A^{O(S)}\log(e/\delta)/\varepsilon^5$ episodes, a number that does not grow with $H$.

Consider stationary [episodic MDPs](episodic-mdp-with-stage-dependent-transitions.md) $M = (\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, R, H, \mu)$: finite state and action sets with $S = |\mathcal{S}|$ and $A = |\mathcal{A}|$, one transition kernel $P$ and one reward distribution $R(s, a)$ on $\mathbb{R}$ for all stages, horizon $H$ and initial distribution $\mu$, where given the history and the current pair $(s_h, a_h) = (s, a)$ the reward $r_h \sim R(s, a)$ and the next state $s_{h+1} \sim P(\cdot \mid s, a)$ are drawn independently, and such that for every policy the rewards $r_0, \dots, r_{H-1}$ of an episode satisfy $r_h \in [0, 1]$ and $\sum_{h=0}^{H-1} r_h \le 1$ almost surely. A learner knows $\mathcal{S}$, $\mathcal{A}$ and $H$ but not $P$, $R$ or $\mu$; it plays episodes as in the stochastic-start [online episodic protocol](online-episodic-learning-and-regret.md), each from an initial state $s_0 \sim \mu$ drawn independently of the past, except that within an episode it may choose each action as a [history-dependent randomized policy](../../../heuristic-search-and-planning/markov-decision-processes/history-dependent-randomized-policies-and-trajectory-laws.md) would, from the data of earlier episodes and the history of the current one; each episode counts in full even if abandoned early. The policy it returns is a non-stationary Markov policy. For a policy $\pi$ let $V^\pi_{M,H} = \mathbb{E}[\sum_{h=0}^{H-1} r_h]$. Let $H_0(S) = 2\ln\big(8(S+1)^{4(S+1)}\big)$. Then there is an algorithm, whose only inputs are $\mathcal{S}$, $\mathcal{A}$, $H$, an accuracy $0 < \varepsilon \le 1/2$ and a confidence parameter $0 < \delta < 1$, such that for every such $\varepsilon$ and $\delta$ and every such MDP $M$ with $H \ge H_0(S)$, with probability at least $1 - \delta$, it returns a non-stationary policy $\pi$ with $V^\pi_{M,H} \ge V^{\pi^\star}_{M,H} - \varepsilon$ after at most

$$
(S+1)^{CS} A^{CS}\cdot\frac{\log(e/\delta)}{\varepsilon^5}
$$

episodes, where $\pi^\star$ is an optimal non-stationary Markov policy (optimal among all policies, randomized and history-dependent ones included) and $C>0$ is an absolute constant fixed with the algorithm, independent of $S$, $A$, $H$, $\varepsilon$, $\delta$ and $M$. All logarithms are natural.

## Expansion

Two assumptions carry the result, and neither can be dropped silently. Stationarity is essential: with stage-dependent transitions the [minimax lower bound](episodic-minimax-regret-lower-bound.md) grows polynomially in $H$. The normalization is total reward at most $1$, so $\varepsilon$ is a fraction of the largest possible return; in the per-step normalization with returns up to $H$ the same accuracy is $H\varepsilon$. The bound also says nothing good about $S$, $A$ or $\varepsilon$: its dependence on them is far worse than that of algorithms whose bounds grow with $H$. The same paper also treats the generative-model setting, where a batch of $H$ queries counts as one unit, with a number of batches polynomial in $S$, $A$ and $1/\varepsilon$ (Theorem 6.3 of the full version).

The comparison class matters. With $H$ finite, the best stationary policy can be much worse than the best non-stationary one: with two states, staying in the first state gives reward $1/(2H)$ per step and leaving gives reward $1/2$ once (after which no reward is collected), so every episode's total reward is at most $1$ as the theorem assumes, the optimal non-stationary value is $1 - 1/(2H)$, and deterministic stationary policies get at most $1/2$. The source states this example with the rewards doubled, outside its own normalization. The proof nevertheless works with stationary policies, of which there are $A^S$. It shows that for $H$ large compared with $S\log S$ there is a stationary policy whose visitation of any state-action pair is within a factor $\exp(O(S\log S))$ of what any non-stationary policy achieves, using a link between finite-horizon and discounted values. It then collects data by running, for each state-action pair $(s, a)$ and each ordered pair of stationary policies $(\pi_1, \pi_2)$, an episode that follows $\pi_1$ until the trajectory first visits $(s, a)$ and $\pi_2$ afterwards (Algorithm 1 of the full version; Algorithm 2 collects in the same way); this switch depends on the history of the episode, which is why the statement allows history-dependent actions during collection. It estimates transitions and rewards to a relative accuracy governed by quantiles of visitation counts, and shows that every non-stationary policy's value in every model consistent with the estimates is at least its true value minus $\varepsilon/2$ (Lemma 5.8), while in one particular consistent model, the true transitions with the estimated rewards, it is at most its true value plus $\varepsilon/2$ (Lemma 5.9). Hence the pessimistic value, the minimum over consistent models, is within $\varepsilon/2$ of the true value for every non-stationary policy (Theorem 5.10), and the policy maximizing the pessimistic value is $\varepsilon$-optimal. Values in a consistent model other than that minimum need not be close to the true value from above. The concentration steps of the full version are multiplicative Chernoff bounds for indicators of quantile events (Lemmas 5.2 and 5.3), Bernstein-type bounds for the transition and reward estimates (Lemma 5.4), and a per-trajectory bound on transition counts proved by orthogonality of martingale increments and Chebyshev's inequality. The stationary-policy comparison uses that some stationary policy is optimal for the discounted value among all policies, applied to the mean rewards.

In Lemma 5.4 the counts are truncated: in each of the $N$ lists of episodes collected by Algorithm 1, only the first $m^{\mathrm{st}}(s, a)$ visits to $(s, a)$ are used, where $m^{\mathrm{st}}$ is the quantile estimate of Algorithm 2, computed from separate episodes and treated as fixed. Let $c_i \in [0, m^{\mathrm{st}}(s, a)]$ be the number of counted visits in list $i$, $m_D = \sum_i c_i$ and $M = \mathbb{E}[m_D \mid m^{\mathrm{st}}]$, the expected count given the calibration data of Algorithm 2, with $m^{\mathrm{st}}$ fixed. The source applies Bernstein's inequality to per-list sums whose range can be as large as $m^{\mathrm{st}}(s, a)$, which can be of order $H$, and it then divides by the lower bound $m_D \ge N\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}} m^{\mathrm{st}}(s, a)/8$. That division gives a first-order term $16\sqrt{PL/(N m^{\mathrm{st}})}/\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}}$, with $L = \log(18S^2A/\delta)$, whereas the source prints $8\sqrt{PL/(N m^{\mathrm{st}} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}})}$, smaller by the factor $2/\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}}}$; the same holds for the rewards. Calibrated against the corrected term, that argument would need a sample size of order $\varepsilon^{-6}$. The repair below recovers the printed shape, with larger absolute constants, by working with the expected count $M$. No counterexample to the theorem is involved.

*Counts.* Given $m^{\mathrm{st}}$, the $c_i/m^{\mathrm{st}}(s, a)$ are independent with values in $[0, 1]$. On the event of Lemma 5.3, $m^{\mathrm{st}}(s, a) \le Q^{\mathrm{st}}_{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}}/4}(s, a)$, and the quantile $Q_\varepsilon(X) = \sup\{x : \Pr[X \ge x] \ge \varepsilon\}$ of an integer-valued $X$ satisfies $\Pr[X \ge Q_\varepsilon(X)] \ge \varepsilon$, so $\mathbb{E}c_i \ge m^{\mathrm{st}}(s, a)\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}}/4$ and $M \ge N\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}} m^{\mathrm{st}}(s, a)/4$. The multiplicative Chernoff bounds give $\Pr[m_D \ge 2M] \le \exp(-M/(3m^{\mathrm{st}}))$ and $\Pr[m_D \le M/2] \le \exp(-M/(8m^{\mathrm{st}}))$, both at most $\exp(-N\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}}/32)$. For Algorithm 3's choices, $N\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}} = 2^{51}(S+1)^{12(S+1)}S^6A^4 L/\varepsilon^4$, so over all pairs these events fail with probability at most $\delta/18$.

*Transitions.* Take the filtration generated by the states and actions of all collected episodes in order, leaving out the rewards; actions in Algorithm 1 depend only on the current state and on whether the pair enumerated for that episode has already occurred in it, so this is a discrete adaptive process. Fix $s'$. The increment $D_t$ at a step is $\mathbb{1}[s_{t+1} = s'] - P(s' \mid s, a)$ if the step is a counted visit to $(s, a)$ and $0$ otherwise. It lies in $[-1, 1]$ and has conditional mean $0$, because given the past states and actions the next state has law $P(\cdot \mid s, a)$; its conditional variance is $P(1 - P)$ times the indicator of a counted visit, so the predictable quadratic variation after all steps is $P(1-P)\, m_D$. Apply [Freedman's maximal inequality](../../../../Probability/concentration-inequalities/adaptive-martingale-tail-bounds/freedman-maximal-inequality.md) with $b = 1$ and the deterministic level $v = 2PM$, to $\sum_t D_t$ and to its negation. On $\{m_D \le 2M\}$ the variation is at most $v$, so with $L' = \log(36S^2A/\delta)$ and $t = 2\sqrt{vL'} + L'$, which satisfies $t^2 \ge 2L'(v + t/3)$, the two-sided failure probability is at most $2e^{-L'}$. A union bound over the $S^2A$ triples puts this at $\delta/18$, with no union bound over the random number of counted visits. On $\{m_D \ge M/2\}$, $\hat P - P = \sum_t D_t/m_D$, hence

$$
|\hat P(s' \mid s, a) - P(s' \mid s, a)| \le \frac{2t}{M} \le 16\sqrt{\frac{P(s' \mid s, a)\, L}{N\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}} m^{\mathrm{st}}(s, a)}} + \frac{16L}{N\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}} m^{\mathrm{st}}(s, a)},
$$

using $M \ge N\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}} m^{\mathrm{st}}/4$ and $L' \le 2L$. This is the source's printed bound with $8$ replaced by $16$. If $P(s' \mid s, a) = 0$, the increments vanish and $\hat P(s' \mid s, a) = 0$. Pairs with $Q^{\mathrm{st}}_{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}}}(s, a) = 0$ are excluded, as in Lemma 5.4. For the others $m^{\mathrm{st}}(s, a) \ge 1$ and $M > 0$.

*Rewards.* The rewards take arbitrary values in $[0, 1]$, which the discrete process above does not cover. Let $Y_i$ be the sum over the counted visits in list $i$ of the centered rewards $r - \mathbb{E}R(s, a)$. The $Y_i$ are independent across lists, which use separate episodes. Each is centered, because the reward at a visit has law $R(s, a)$ given the history and counting is predictable. By orthogonality of these increments, $\mathbb{E}Y_i^2 \le \mathbb{E}[R(s, a)^2]\,\mathbb{E}c_i$.

Their range does not grow with $H$. If no policy visits $(s, a)$ twice in one episode with positive probability, a list counts at most one visit in each of its $SA \cdot A^{2S}$ episodes, so $|Y_i| \le S A^{2S+1}$. Otherwise Lemma 2.1 gives $R(s, a) \le 2S/H$ almost surely, and a list has at most $SA \cdot A^{2S} \cdot H$ steps, so $|Y_i| \le 2S^2A^{2S+1}$. Lemma 2.1 uses $H \ge S$, the bound on total reward and, in its proof, that the reward at each visit is drawn from $R(s, a)$ independently of the next state, as assumed above. Thus $|Y_i| \le b = 2S^2A^{2S+1}$ in both cases.

If $\mathbb{E}[R(s, a)^2] = 0$, the rewards vanish almost surely and the estimate is exact. Otherwise take $\sigma_i^2 = \mathbb{E}[R(s, a)^2]\,\mathbb{E}c_i$:

- The bound on the moment generating function of a bounded variable gives $\mathbb{E}e^{tY_i} \le \exp(t^2\sigma_i^2/(2(1 - b|t|))) \le \exp(t^2\sigma_i^2)$ for $2b|t| < 1$. Since $Y_i$ is bounded, $e^{tY_i}$ is integrable, so $Y_i$ is sub-exponential with parameters $(\sqrt2\sigma_i, 2b)$.
- The sum over the $N$ lists is sub-exponential with parameters $(\sqrt{2\mathbb{E}[R(s, a)^2]M}, 2b)$.
- The two tail regimes and negation give $\Pr[|\sum_i Y_i| \ge t] \le 2\exp(-\min\{t^2/(4\mathbb{E}[R(s, a)^2]M),\ t/(4b)\})$.

With $L'' = \log(18SA/\delta)$ and $t = 2\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[R(s, a)^2] M L''} + 4bL''$, a union bound over pairs gives failure probability $\delta/9$. On $\{m_D \ge M/2\}$, using $m^{\mathrm{st}} \ge Q^{\mathrm{st}}_{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}}}$, the reward estimate is then within

$$
8\sqrt{\frac{\mathbb{E}[R(s, a)^2]\,L''}{N\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}} Q^{\mathrm{st}}_{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}}}(s, a)}} + \frac{32bL''}{N\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}} Q^{\mathrm{st}}_{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}}}(s, a)}
$$

of $\mathbb{E}R(s, a)$. This is the source's printed shape with the second-order term larger by the factor $4b$.

*Initial distribution.* The empirical initial distribution is an average of $N$ independent indicators. When $0 < \mu(s) < 1$ it is handled by the same chain with $b = 1$; when $\mu(s) \in \{0, 1\}$ the centered indicators vanish and the estimate is exact.

*Budget and constants.* These events, with Lemmas 5.2 and 5.3 at $\delta/3$ each and the initial-distribution event at $\delta/9$, fail with total probability at most $\delta$. Every candidate model of Algorithm 3 (line 7) uses the estimated rewards $\hat R$. Enlarging the transition and initial-distribution widths of Algorithm 3 by absolute factors keeps the model $(P, \hat R, \mu)$ among the candidates; the reward accuracy enters Lemma 5.8 for every candidate and Lemma 5.9 for that model; and enlarging $N$ by an absolute factor times $b$ restores the accuracies that Lemmas 5.8 and 5.9 require. The dependence on $\varepsilon$ stays $\varepsilon^{-5}$, and the factor $O(S^2A^{2S+1})$ is absorbed into $(S+1)^{CS}A^{CS}$ by enlarging the absolute constant $C$. The sample sizes printed in the source's Algorithms 2 and 3 are calibrated to its own constants.

A good expansion adds pages for the steps of the proof in the full version, arXiv:2111.00633v1, none of which the graph states:

- Lemma 2.1: under the bounded-total-reward assumption and $H \ge S$, a state-action pair that some policy visits more than once in an episode with positive probability has reward at most $2S/H$ almost surely.
- The comparison of stationary and non-stationary policies through discounted values: the reaching-probability Lemmas 4.1 to 4.4 for Markov chains, Lemma 4.5 (finite-horizon against discounted values of a stationary policy for $H \ge 2\ln(8S^{4S})$), Lemma 4.6, and Corollaries 4.7 to 4.9 (some stationary policy has value, and visitation quantiles, within factors $\exp(O(S\log S))$ of the best non-stationary ones).
- The data collection and quantile estimation of Section 5.1: Lemmas 5.1 to 5.4.
- The perturbation analysis of Section 5.2: Lemma 5.5, the per-trajectory visit bounds Lemmas 5.6 and 5.7, Lemma 5.8 (in every model consistent with the estimates, every policy's value is at least its true value minus the accuracy parameter) and Lemma 5.9 (in a model that keeps the true transitions and initial distribution and whose mean rewards meet the estimation bounds, every policy's value is at most its true value plus the accuracy parameter); the proof of Theorem 5.10 applies both with accuracy $\varepsilon/2$, Lemma 5.9 only to the model with the estimated rewards.
- The pessimistic planning of Section 5.3: Theorem 5.10 (an $\varepsilon/2$-accurate value oracle for every non-stationary policy) and Corollary 5.11, the formal statement of the theorem above.

The episode count includes both the main sampling phase of Algorithm 3 and the preliminary quantile estimation of Algorithm 2, with the sample counts rounded up to integers. The final display of the proof of Theorem 5.10 drops the preliminary term; the statement above gives the asymptotic order obtained by counting both phases, not the printed exact leading constant. There is also a logarithm discrepancy: Algorithm 3 and the confidence intervals of Lemma 5.4 use $\log(18S^2A/\delta)$, whereas the statements of Theorem 5.10 and Corollary 5.11 print $\log(12S^2A/\delta)$. The informal Theorem I.1 (Theorem 1.1 of the full version) writes $\log(1/\delta)$, which is not a uniform bound as $\delta$ approaches $1$. The stated form instead uses $\log(e/\delta)$: $\log(18S^2A/\delta) \le \log(18S^2A)\log(e/\delta)$, and the remaining factors and integer-rounding overhead are absorbed into $(S+1)^{CS}A^{CS}$ for an absolute $C$. No numerical value of $C$ is claimed.

The ranges of the parameters are those of the formal proof. The perturbation Lemmas 5.8 and 5.9 assume $0 < \varepsilon \le 1/2$; for $\varepsilon \ge 1$ every policy is $\varepsilon$-optimal, since values lie in $[0, 1]$. The full version assumes $H = \Omega(S\log S)$ throughout, and its Lemma 4.5 and Corollary 4.7 need $H \ge 2\ln(8S^{4S})$ and its Corollary 4.8 needs $H \ge H_0(S)$; $H_0(S)$ also exceeds $2S$, as Lemma 4.6 needs, and $S$, as Lemma 2.1 needs. For smaller $H$ the source refers to existing algorithms, citing Zhang, Ji and Du, whose Theorem 1 bounds the regret of their Monotonic Value Propagation algorithm, under the same total-reward assumption, by $O(\sqrt{SAK} + S^2A)$ times a polylogarithmic factor in $SAHK/\delta$, with probability at least $1 - \delta$. Their conversion to an $\varepsilon$-optimal policy (Section 2 of their paper) bounds the expected suboptimality of the policy of a uniformly chosen episode; it gives neither $\varepsilon$-optimality with probability at least $1 - \delta$ nor a dependence linear in $\log(1/\delta)$. The short-horizon case of the informal theorem is therefore not claimed here.
