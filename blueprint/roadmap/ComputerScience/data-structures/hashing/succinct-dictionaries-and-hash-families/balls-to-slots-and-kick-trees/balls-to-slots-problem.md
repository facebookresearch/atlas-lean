# Balls-to-slots problem and probe complexity

In the *balls-to-slots* (probe-complexity) problem there are $M$ slots of capacity one, and each ball $x$ of a universe has a fixed *probe sequence* $p_x(0), p_x(1), \dots$ of slots determined by hashing. An oblivious adversary, who does not know the hash functions, fixes in advance a sequence of ball insertions and deletions such that at most $(1 - \varepsilon) M + 1$ balls are present at a time, for a load-factor parameter $\varepsilon \in [0, 1)$. As in the source, the scheme also has $M$ extra *special* slots: an inserted ball is first placed in a special slot, and the scheme may then move it and other balls. A balls-to-slots scheme maintains an assignment of the present balls to distinct slots in which each ball $x$ sits in a slot of its probe sequence or in a special slot, so the up to $(1 - \varepsilon) M + 1$ present balls, which exceed $M$ when $\varepsilon < 1/M$, always fit. If $x$ sits in slot $p_x(i)$, with $i$ the least such index, its *probe complexity* is $1 + \lfloor \log_2 (i + 1) \rfloor$; the source defines it as $\Theta(1 + \log i)$ for one-based indices $i$, so this choice fixes a representative up to constant factors. A ball in a special slot has probe complexity $1 + \lfloor \log_2 M \rfloor$ (the source's $\log M$). The scheme is measured by the average (equivalently, total) probe complexity of the present balls and by its *switching cost*: the number of balls it moves on an insertion or deletion, including the ball inserted. In an augmented open-addressed hash table, the probe complexity of a key is, up to constant factors, the number of query-router bits needed to locate the key, and the switching cost is a lower bound on the time of an insertion or deletion.
