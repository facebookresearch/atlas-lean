# Holenstein's local embedding lemma

A distribution $P_0$ of pairs $(x, y)$ is *$\varepsilon$-embeddable* in a distribution $P_1$ of $((x,y),(s,t))$ if Alice, holding $x$, and Bob, holding $y$, can use shared randomness and no communication to output $s$ and $t$ so that $((x,y),(s,t))$ is within statistical distance $\varepsilon$ of $P_1$. Let $J$ be a joint distribution of $((X, Y), S)$ with conditional laws $P_{S|XY}$, $P_{S|X}$, $P_{S|Y}$, and let $\varepsilon_1, \varepsilon_2$ be the distances of $J$ from $P_{XY}P_{S|X}$ and $P_{XY}P_{S|Y}$. Then $P_{XY}$ is $(2\varepsilon_1 + 2\varepsilon_2)$-embeddable in the law of $((X, Y), (S, S))$: Alice and Bob run correlated sampling on $P_{S|X=x}$ and $P_{S|Y=y}$. For another input distribution $P_0$ of pairs, measuring $\varepsilon_1, \varepsilon_2$ against $P_0P_{S|X}$ and $P_0P_{S|Y}$, $P_0$ is $(3\varepsilon_1 + 2\varepsilon_2)$-embeddable in the law of $((X, Y), (S, S))$. For a joint distribution of $((X, Y), (S, T))$ in which $S$ is independent of $(Y, T)$ given $X$, and $T$ is independent of $(X, S)$ given $Y$ (Markov chains $S \leftrightarrow X \leftrightarrow YT$ and $XS \leftrightarrow Y \leftrightarrow T$), $P_{XY}$ is $0$-embeddable in the law of $((X, Y), (S, T))$, with private randomness only.

## Proof sketch

For fixed $(x, y)$ the two local samples are each within their kernel distances of $P_{S|XY}$, and correlated sampling makes them disagree with probability at most twice the distance between the two local kernels. Averaging over $(x, y)$ and the triangle inequality give $2\varepsilon_1 + 2\varepsilon_2$ (Holenstein, Lemma 5.2). For the changed input distribution, the first hypothesis gives $\|P_{XY} - P_0\| \le \varepsilon_1$, so $(X, Y)$ and a sample of $P_0$ can be coupled to agree with probability at least $1 - \varepsilon_1$, and the same local functions give $3\varepsilon_1 + 2\varepsilon_2$ (Corollary 5.3). Under the two Markov conditions, Alice samples $S$ from $P_{S|X=x}$ and Bob samples $T$ from $P_{T|Y=y}$ with private coins, and $P_{XYST} = P_{XY}P_{S|X}P_{T|Y}$ is exactly the law they produce (Lemma 5.4).

Three elementary properties follow from the definition, with statistical distance as the distance. Monotonicity: an $\varepsilon$-embedding is an $\varepsilon'$-embedding for every $\varepsilon' \ge \varepsilon$, since the same protocol works. Maps: if $P_0$ is $\varepsilon$-embeddable in the law of $((X, Y), (S, T))$ and $g, h$ are functions, then $P_0$ is $\varepsilon$-embeddable in the law of $((X, Y), (g(X, S), h(Y, T)))$: Alice outputs $g(x, s)$ and Bob $h(y, t)$, and the map $(x, y, s, t) \mapsto (x, y, g(x, s), h(y, t))$ does not increase statistical distance. Transitivity: if $P_0$ is $\varepsilon$-embeddable in the law $P_1$ of $((X, Y), (S, T))$ and the law of $((X, S), (Y, T))$ under $P_1$, viewed as a distribution of pairs, is $\varepsilon'$-embeddable in a law $P_2$ of $(((X, S), (Y, T)), (S', T'))$, then running the two protocols in sequence with independent shared randomness embeds $P_0$ in the law of $((X, Y), (S', T'))$ under $P_2$ with error at most $\varepsilon + \varepsilon'$: applying the second protocol to the output of the first is within $\varepsilon$ of applying it to $P_1$ (the protocol is a fixed channel, which does not increase statistical distance), which is within $\varepsilon'$ of $P_2$, and forgetting $(S, T)$ is a map.
