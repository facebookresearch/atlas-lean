# The Raft consensus protocol

Raft replicates a log of state-machine commands on a fixed finite cluster of servers. Servers fail by stopping and may later recover from stable storage; messages may be delayed, lost, duplicated or reordered. The protocol is a labelled transition system whose states consist of the state of every server and the multiset of messages in flight, and whose executions start from the state in which every server is in its initial state and no message is in flight. A *majority* is a set of more than half of the servers.

Each server is up or crashed and keeps

- *persistent state*, which survives crashes: its current term `currentTerm`, the server `votedFor` that received its vote in the current term (or none), and its log, a sequence of entries indexed from $1$, each holding a command and the term in which the leader received it;
- *volatile state*, lost in a crash: its role (*follower*, *candidate* or *leader*), the index `commitIndex` of the highest entry known to be committed, the index `lastApplied` of the highest entry applied to its state machine, for every server the index `nextIndex` of the next entry to send to it and the index `matchIndex` of the highest entry known to be replicated on it (used by a leader), and the set `votesGranted` of servers that granted it their vote in its current term (used by a candidate).

Initially a server is up, in term $0$, has voted for no one and has an empty log; it is a follower with `commitIndex` and `lastApplied` equal to $0$, every `nextIndex` equal to $1$, every `matchIndex` equal to $0$ and no votes. The *last index* of a log is its length, and its *last term* is the term of its last entry, $0$ for an empty log. Of two logs whose last entries have different terms the one with the later term is more *up to date*; if the last terms are equal the longer log is more up to date.

A message has a sender and a receiver. A *RequestVote* request carries the candidate's term and the index and term of its last entry; a *RequestVote* response carries the responder's term and whether it grants its vote. An *AppendEntries* request carries the leader's term, the index $p$ and the term of the entry preceding the new ones ($0$ when $p = 0$), the new entries and the leader's `commitIndex`; an *AppendEntries* response carries the responder's term, whether it succeeds and, on success, the match index $p$ plus the number of new entries ($0$ on failure). The sender of a request is its candidate or leader.

The steps are as follows; every event of a server other than its recovery requires the server to be up.

- *Election timeout*: a follower or candidate increments `currentTerm`, becomes a candidate, votes for itself (setting `votedFor` to itself and `votesGranted` to itself alone) and sends every other server a RequestVote request with its new term and the index and term of its last entry.
- *Election*: a candidate whose `votesGranted` is a majority becomes leader and sets, for every server, `nextIndex` to its own last log index plus one and `matchIndex` to $0$.
- *Client request*: a leader appends an entry with term `currentTerm` and an arbitrary command to its log.
- *Replication*: a leader sends another server $j$ an AppendEntries request with its current term, the preceding index $p$ = `nextIndex`$[j] - 1$ and the term of its entry at $p$ ($0$ if there is none), the first $k$ entries of its log after $p$ for some $k \ge 0$ (all of them if there are fewer; none for a heartbeat), and its `commitIndex`.
- *Commitment*: a leader sets `commitIndex` to $N$ when $N$ exceeds `commitIndex`, its log has an entry at $N$ whose term is `currentTerm`, and a majority of the servers $j$ have `matchIndex`$[j] \ge N$, the leader counting itself with its last log index in place of a `matchIndex`.
- *Application*: a server whose `commitIndex` exceeds `lastApplied` and whose log has an entry at index `lastApplied` $+ 1$ increments `lastApplied`, applying that entry's command; the event records the server, the index and the command.
- *Receipt*: the receiver takes one copy of a message out of the network and handles it as follows; its reply, if any, enters the network.
  - *All servers*: if the message carries a term $T$ larger than `currentTerm`, the receiver first sets `currentTerm` to $T$, resets `votedFor` to none, and becomes a follower.
  - *RequestVote*: the receiver grants its vote, setting `votedFor` to the candidate, if the request's term is its `currentTerm`, `votedFor` is none or this candidate, and the candidate's log, as described by the index and term of its last entry, is at least as up to date as its own; it replies with `currentTerm` and whether it granted its vote.
  - *AppendEntries*: if the term is below its `currentTerm` the receiver replies false. Otherwise a leader ignores the request, and a follower or candidate becomes a follower and replies false if its log has no entry at the preceding index with the preceding term (index $0$ always matches). Otherwise it deletes the first existing entry that conflicts with a new one (same index, different term) together with all that follow it, appends the new entries not already in its log, and, if the leader's `commitIndex` exceeds its own, sets `commitIndex` to the minimum of the leader's `commitIndex` and the match index (Figure 2's "index of last new entry", read as the preceding index for a request with no entries); it replies true with the match index.
  - *RequestVote response*: a candidate adds the sender to `votesGranted` if the response grants the vote and carries its `currentTerm`.
  - *AppendEntries response*: a leader, if the response carries its `currentTerm`, on success with match index $k$ sets the sender's `nextIndex` to $k + 1$ and its `matchIndex` to the larger of its `matchIndex` and $k$, and on failure decrements the sender's `nextIndex`, but not below $1$.
  - Every other response is ignored.
- *Network*: a copy of a message in flight is lost, or a message in flight is duplicated. Since any message in flight may be received at any time, messages are also delayed and reordered.
- *Crash and recovery*: a server crashes, keeping its persistent state while its volatile state takes its initial values, and a crashed server recovers.

This is the protocol of Figure 2 without cluster membership changes or log compaction, with these conventions.

- Timing is abstracted away: an election timeout may occur at any moment at a follower or candidate that is up. In the source a follower becomes a candidate only when its election timeout elapses without an AppendEntries from the current leader and without granting a vote; this, like the timer itself, only constrains when timeouts occur.
- A candidate sends its RequestVote requests when its election starts and does not resend them. A resend, as servers retry RPCs that get no timely response (Section 5.1), would equal the first request, so from the receiver's point of view it is matched by the network delaying the first request, or duplicating it before any copy is lost and delaying the copy; once the only copy of a request is lost, the network cannot recreate it. A leader's initial heartbeats are replication steps with $k = 0$.
- Clients only submit commands to leaders; replies to clients and redirection by followers are left out. The state machine is left out too: applying an entry is an observable event, and a recovered server, whose `lastApplied` is $0$, applies its entries again from index $1$.
- Responses name their sender and carry the match index in place of the context of a remote procedure call. With the term rule applied first, a response is acted on only by a candidate or leader whose `currentTerm` it carries, and a leader does not act on an AppendEntries request of its own term. Steps are atomic, so persistent state is on stable storage before any reply is sent.
- On success a leader sets `matchIndex` to the larger of its value and the reported match index. Taking the maximum preserves the monotonicity Figure 2 requires between elections, where `matchIndex` is initialized to $0$, increases monotonically and is reinitialized after each election; the authors' TLA+ specification instead assigns the reported index. Without the context of a call, a leader re-elected in a later term can receive a refusal of one of its requests of an earlier term; the refusal carries the responder's current term, which is now the leader's, so the leader lowers that server's `nextIndex` even when the two logs agree.
- The update of a follower's `commitIndex` is Figure 2's, taken literally. It lowers `commitIndex`, which Figure 2 describes as increasing, on any accepted request whose leader `commitIndex` exceeds the follower's but whose match index is below it: an old or duplicated request, or a fresh one with fewer entries, such as a heartbeat sent while `nextIndex` lags. A crash also resets `commitIndex` and `lastApplied` to $0$.

The authors' TLA+ specification shares these conventions for the network, the responses and restarts: messages form a bag that the network may duplicate or drop, responses name their sender and carry the match index, responses with a stale term are discarded, a leader does not handle an AppendEntries request of its own term, and a restart keeps only `currentTerm`, `votedFor` and the log. It also notes that its version of the update can lower a follower's `commitIndex` on an old, duplicated request.
