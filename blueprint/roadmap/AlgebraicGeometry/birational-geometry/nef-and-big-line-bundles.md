# Nef and big line bundles

Let $X$ be an integral scheme projective over a field $k$, not necessarily geometrically reduced
over $k$, and $L$ an invertible sheaf on $X$. If $H^0(X, L^\nu) \ne 0$ for some $\nu \ge 1$, the
sections of $L^\nu$ define a rational map $\varphi_\nu \colon X \dashrightarrow \mathbb{P}(H^0(X,
L^\nu))$. The *Iitaka dimension* of $L$ is $\kappa(L) = -\infty$ if $H^0(X, L^\nu) = 0$ for all $\nu
\ge 1$, and otherwise

$$\kappa(L) = \max\{\dim \varphi_\nu(X) : \nu \ge 1,\ H^0(X, L^\nu) \ne 0\};$$

it lies in $\{-\infty, 0, 1, \dots, \dim X\}$. The sheaf $L$ is *big* if $\kappa(L) = \dim X$, and
*nef* (numerically effective) if $\deg(L|_C) \ge 0$ for every irreducible curve $C \subset X$, that
is, if the intersection number $(L \cdot C)$ is non-negative for every such curve. A Cartier divisor
$B$, for instance any divisor when $X$ is smooth, is called nef or big if $\mathcal{O}_X(B)$ is.
Lazarsfeld extends both notions "in the evident manner" to $\mathbb{Q}$-divisors $D$ on an integral
smooth projective variety: $D$ is nef if $(D \cdot C) \ge 0$ for every irreducible curve $C$, the
intersection number being extended $\mathbb{Q}$-linearly, and big if some positive multiple of $D$
is an integral big divisor.

Bigness is maximal growth of the spaces of sections, which is how Lazarsfeld phrases it ($h^0(X,
\mathcal{O}_X(mB))$ grows like $m^{\dim X}$): if $H^0(X, L^\nu) \ne 0$ for some $\nu \ge 1$, there
are real numbers $a, b > 0$ with

$$a\,\mu^{\kappa(L)} \le \dim H^0(X, L^{\nu\mu}) \le b\,\mu^{\kappa(L)} \qquad (\mu \ge 1)$$

(Esnault–Viehweg, Properties 5.4 b)). Moreover $\kappa(L) = \dim X$ if and only if $L^\nu(-C)$ is
ample for some $\nu \ge 1$ and some effective **Cartier** divisor $C$ (Properties 5.4 d), whose
proof constructs $C$ from a nonzero section of $L^m(-A)$ for an ample $A$); on a singular $X$ an
effective Weil divisor need not do, as the ruling $x = z = 0$ of the quadric cone $xy = z^2$ in
$\mathbb{P}^3$ is not Cartier at the vertex. Integrality is needed: on $\mathbb{P}^1 \sqcup
\mathbb{P}^1$, the sheaf $L = \mathcal{O}(1) \sqcup \mathcal{O}$ is generated by its sections and
$\kappa(L) = 1 = \dim X$, but $L^\nu(-C)$ has degree at most $0$ on the second line for every
effective Cartier divisor $C$, so it is never ample. The bounds of 5.4 b) hold along the multiples
of a $\nu$ with $H^0(X, L^\nu) \ne 0$, not along all $m$: a nontrivial torsion line bundle of degree
$0$ on an elliptic curve has sections only in degrees divisible by its order.

**Analytic definitions.** Let $X$ be a compact complex manifold with a hermitian metric $\omega$ and
$L$ a holomorphic line bundle on $X$. Demailly calls $L$ nef if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is
a smooth hermitian metric $h_\varepsilon$ on $L$ whose curvature satisfies
$i\Theta_{L,h_\varepsilon} \ge -\varepsilon\omega$ (Definition 6.11). He defines the Kodaira–Iitaka
dimension $\kappa(L)$ as the supremum over $m \ge 1$ of the ranks of the maps

$$\Phi_m \colon X \setminus B_m \to \mathbb{P}(V_m), \qquad x \mapsto \{\sigma \in V_m : \sigma(x) = 0\},$$

where $V_m = H^0(X, mL)$ and $B_m = \bigcap_{\sigma \in V_m} \sigma^{-1}(0)$ is its base locus, with
$\kappa(L) = -\infty$ if $V_m = 0$ for all $m \ge 1$; $L$ is big if $\kappa(L) = \dim X$ (Definition
6.12). On a projective manifold carrying an ample line bundle $A$, the following are equivalent: $L$
is nef in the sense of curves; $kL + A$ is ample for every integer $k \ge 1$; $L$ is nef in the
analytic sense, a condition that does not depend on $\omega$ (Proposition 6.10, whose proof uses the
Nakai–Moishezon criterion for ampleness). The first condition gives the second by the consequence of
Seshadri's criterion that $L^k \otimes A$ is ample, not by Nakai's criterion alone; the second gives
the third because the ample $kL + A$ carries a metric of positive curvature, pulled back from a
Fubini–Study metric by a very ample power, whence metrics on $L$ with curvature $\ge -\omega/k$ for
$\omega$ the curvature of $A$; and the third gives the first by integrating the curvature over
curves and letting $\varepsilon \to 0$.
