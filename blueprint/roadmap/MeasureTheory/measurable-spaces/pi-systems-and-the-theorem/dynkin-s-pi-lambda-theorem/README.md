# Dynkin's π–λ theorem

Let $\mathcal{P}$ be a π-system of subsets of $\alpha$, closed under nonempty intersections, that generates the
σ-algebra of $\alpha$. A property of measurable sets that holds for $\emptyset$ and for the sets of $\mathcal{P}$, and
is preserved by complements and by countable disjoint unions, holds for every measurable set. Equivalently, a λ-system
containing $\mathcal{P}$, a family that contains $\alpha$ and is closed under complements and countable disjoint unions,
contains the σ-algebra generated by $\mathcal{P}$: apply the theorem to membership in it.

## Idea

Complements and countable disjoint unions are what countable additivity controls, but intersections are not, so the sets
on which two finite measures with the same total mass agree form a λ-system rather than a σ-algebra. Finiteness is
needed for complements: counting measure and twice counting measure on $\mathbb{N}$ agree on $\mathbb{N}$ and on
$\mathbb{N} \setminus \{1\}$, but not on $\{1\}$. By the theorem, two finite measures with the same total mass that
agree on a π-system agree on the σ-algebra it generates, so a probability measure on $\mathbb{R}$ is determined by its
distribution function, and independence of σ-algebras reduces to independence of generating π-systems.
