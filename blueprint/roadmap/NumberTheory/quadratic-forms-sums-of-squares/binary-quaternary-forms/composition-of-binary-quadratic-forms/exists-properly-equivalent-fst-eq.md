# A primitively represented integer is the first coefficient of a properly equivalent form

Let $f = (a, b, c)$ be a form and let $x_0, y_0$ be integers with $\gcd(x_0, y_0) = 1$, and put
$n = a x_0^2 + b x_0 y_0 + c y_0^2$. Then there are integers $b'$ and $c'$ such that $(n, b', c')$
is properly equivalent to $(a, b, c)$, and $(n, b', c')$ has the discriminant of $(a, b, c)$.

## Sources

- Anthony W. Knapp, *Advanced Algebra*, digital second edition, 2016, Chapter I, §3, p. 18, the proof of uniqueness in Theorem 1.6(b) (a matrix of determinant $1$ with first column $(x_0, y_0)$ carries the form to one taking the represented value at $(1, 0)$), and §4, p. 28, the proof of Theorem 1.12(a), which applies this argument to arbitrary primitively represented integers.
