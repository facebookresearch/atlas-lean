# Isomorphism of finitely presented associative algebras over the integers is undecidable

No algorithm decides whether two finitely presented, possibly noncommutative, algebras over the integers are isomorphic; for finitely generated commutative rings the question is open.

Let $\mathbb{Z}\langle x_1, \dots, x_n \rangle$ be the free associative algebra with $1$ over $\mathbb{Z}$ in $n$ noncommuting variables. A *finitely presented* $\mathbb{Z}$-algebra is a quotient

$$
\mathbb{Z}\langle x_1, \dots, x_n \rangle / (f_1, \dots, f_m)
$$

by the two-sided ideal generated by finitely many elements $f_1, \dots, f_m$; it is given by $n$ and the relations $f_1, \dots, f_m$, each written as a noncommutative polynomial: a finite list of terms $c\,w$, with $c$ a nonzero integer and $w$ a word in $x_1, \dots, x_n$ (the empty word standing for $1$). Isomorphism means isomorphism of rings with identity.

**Theorem.** There is no algorithm that decides, given two finite presentations of $\mathbb{Z}$-algebras $A$ and $B$, whether $A \cong B$.

Conventions: for commutative rings the corresponding problem, whether two finitely generated commutative rings $\mathbb{Z}[x_1, \dots, x_n]/(f_1, \dots, f_m)$ are isomorphic, has unknown status, as has the isomorphism problem for finitely generated commutative algebras over any fixed nonzero commutative ring whose elements are encoded so that addition and multiplication are computable.

## Expansion

For a finitely presented group $G = \langle g_1, \dots, g_k \mid r_1, \dots, r_l \rangle$, the integral group ring $\mathbb{Z}G$ is a finitely presented $\mathbb{Z}$-algebra, with generators $x_i, y_i$ for $g_i$ and $g_i^{-1}$ and relations $x_i y_i = y_i x_i = 1$ and $r_j(x, y) = 1$, computable from the group presentation. The augmentation $\mathbb{Z}G \to \mathbb{Z}$ is an isomorphism when $G$ is trivial, and $\mathbb{Z}G$ is a free abelian group of rank $|G|$, so $\mathbb{Z}G \cong \mathbb{Z}$ exactly when $G = \{1\}$. An algorithm for isomorphism would therefore decide whether a finitely presented group is trivial, contradicting the Adian–Rabin theorem. The argument only compares algebras with $\mathbb{Z}$.
