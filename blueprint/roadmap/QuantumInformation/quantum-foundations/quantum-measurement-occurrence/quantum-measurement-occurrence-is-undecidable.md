# Quantum measurement occurrence is undecidable for nine outcomes in dimension 15

No algorithm decides, given the rational Kraus operators of a quantum measurement repeated on its own output, whether some finite sequence of outcomes can never be observed, even from a full-rank input state. This holds already for measurements with 9 outcomes on a 15-dimensional system.

**Setting.** A measurement on $\mathbb{C}^d$ with $K$ outcomes is given by Kraus operators $A_1, \dots, A_K \in \mathbb{C}^{d \times d}$ with $\sum_{j=1}^{K} A_j^\dagger A_j = \mathbb{1}$, a [measurement](../../quantum-operations/povms-and-measurement-readout/povm-and-born-rule.md) with POVM elements $A_j^\dagger A_j$. Outcome $j$ occurs with probability $\operatorname{tr}(A_j \rho A_j^\dagger)$ and changes the state to $A_j \rho A_j^\dagger / \operatorname{tr}(A_j \rho A_j^\dagger)$. Feeding the output into an identical device $n$ times, the sequence $j_1, \dots, j_n$ occurs with probability

$$
p_{j_1 \cdots j_n} = \operatorname{tr}\bigl(A_{j_n} \cdots A_{j_1}\, \rho\, A_{j_1}^\dagger \cdots A_{j_n}^\dagger\bigr).
$$

**Problem.** The *quantum measurement occurrence problem* asks, given Kraus operators $A_1, \dots, A_K \in \mathbb{Q}^{d \times d}$ of such a measurement, whether some finite sequence of outcomes $j_1, \dots, j_n$ can never be observed, even if the input state has full rank. For a full-rank $\rho$, $p_{j_1 \cdots j_n} = 0$ exactly when $A_{j_n} \cdots A_{j_1} = 0$, so the question is whether the semigroup generated by the Kraus operators contains the zero matrix.

**Theorem (Eisert, Mueller and Gogolin).** The quantum measurement occurrence problem for $K = 9$ and $d = 15$ is undecidable.

By contrast, the source proves the corresponding classical problem decidable: there a selective measurement is a decomposition of a stochastic matrix into $K$ matrices with nonnegative entries.

## Expansion

The proof reduces the matrix mortality problem for eight integer $3 \times 3$ matrices, which the source quotes as undecidable (Paterson; Halava and Harju). Given $M_1, \dots, M_8$, multiplying them on the right by the sign matrices $\mathrm{diag}(-1,1,1)$, $\mathrm{diag}(1,-1,1)$ and $\mathrm{diag}(1,1,-1)$ gives 32 matrices whose sum $\sum_j M_j^\dagger M_j$ is diagonal. Lagrange's four-square theorem then supplies four integer diagonal matrices completing the sum to $c^2 \mathbb{1}_3$ for an integer $c$. For $j \leq 8$ let $A_j$ be $\tfrac{4}{5c}$ times the $15 \times 15$ matrix whose first three columns stack $M_j, M_{8+j}, M_{16+j}, M_{24+j}, M_{32+j}$ (with $M_{37}, \dots, M_{40} = 0$) and whose other columns vanish, and let $A_9 = \tfrac{3}{5}\mathbb{1}_3 \oplus \mathbb{1}_{12}$. Then $\sum_{j=1}^{9} A_j^\dagger A_j = \mathbb{1}_{15}$. If $M_{j_n} \cdots M_{j_1} = 0$, the product $A_{j_n} \cdots A_{j_1}$ is strictly lower triangular, hence nilpotent, so some power of it vanishes and the corresponding outcome sequence never occurs. Conversely, if $A_{j_n} \cdots A_{j_1} = 0$, deleting the letters $9$ from the sequence leaves a word whose product of the $M_j$ vanishes.
