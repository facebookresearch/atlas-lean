# Many-to-one stable matching reduces to one-to-one by quota slots

Consider a college-admissions market with finitely many students and colleges. Each student has a strict ranking of the colleges and of remaining unmatched; each college $c$ has a quota $q_c \ge 1$ and a strict ranking of the students and of leaving a seat empty (a student ranked above an empty seat is acceptable). A matching assigns each student to at most one college and each college $c$ at most $q_c$ students. It is stable if every student finds the assigned college acceptable, every college finds each of its students acceptable, and no student $s$ and college $c$ are such that $s$ prefers $c$ to the assignment of $s$ while $c$ either has fewer than $q_c$ students and finds $s$ acceptable or prefers $s$ to one of its students; this pairwise definition is the one used when colleges' preferences over sets of students are responsive to these rankings. In the copies market each college $c$ is replaced by $q_c$ copies $c^1, \dots, c^{q_c}$ that rank students as $c$ does and accept one student each, and every student ranks the copies of $c$ consecutively, in the order $c^1, c^2, \dots$, where $c$ stood. Then (i) in every stable matching of the copies market the occupied copies of each $c$ are $c^1, \dots, c^r$ for some $r$, holding students in decreasing order of $c$'s ranking; and (ii) a matching $\mu$ of the college market is stable if and only if the copies matching $\mu'$ that gives the students of $c$ to $c^1, c^2, \dots$ in $c$'s order is stable. By (i) every stable copies matching is $\mu'$ for exactly one $\mu$, so $\mu \mapsto \mu'$ is a bijection between the two sets of stable matchings. A college with quota $0$ holds no student and is in no blocking pair, so deleting it changes neither the matchings nor their stability; the correspondence therefore also covers quotas $q_c \ge 0$, with no copies for a college of quota $0$.

Proof. (i) If $c^j$ held $s$ and $c^k$ held $s'$ with $j < k$ and $c$ preferring $s'$ to $s$, then $s'$ prefers $c^j$ to $c^k$ and $c^j$ prefers $s'$ to $s$, a blocking pair; if $c^j$ were empty while $c^k$ held $s$, then $s$ prefers $c^j$ and $c^j$ finds $s$ acceptable, again blocking. (ii) Acceptability transfers in both directions. Suppose $(s, c^j)$ blocks $\mu'$. If $s$ holds a copy $c^k$ of $c$, then $j < k$, so by construction $c^j$ holds a student whom $c$ ranks above $s$, contradicting that $c^j$ prefers $s$ to its student. Otherwise $s$ prefers $c$ to the assignment of $s$, and either $c^j$ is empty, so $c$ has fewer than $q_c$ students and finds $s$ acceptable, or $c$ prefers $s$ to the student of $c^j$; either way $(s, c)$ blocks $\mu$. Conversely, if $(s, c)$ blocks $\mu$, then either $c$ has $r < q_c$ students and $(s, c^{r+1})$ blocks $\mu'$, or $c$ prefers $s$ to a student held by some $c^j$ and $(s, c^j)$ blocks $\mu'$.

Existence of a stable matching of the college market, and stability and student-optimality of the student-proposing deferred-acceptance outcome, follow from the corresponding one-to-one results for markets with unacceptable partners and sides of different sizes; those are separate statements.
