# Leaf trees and branch trees are disjoint

For a context-free grammar with nonterminals $N$ and terminals $\Sigma$, a derivation tree is either a leaf labelled by a nonterminal $A$ and a terminal $a$ (standing for the rule $A \to a$), or a node labelled by nonterminals $A, B, C$ with a left and a right subtree (standing for the rule $A \to B\,C$). The root of a tree is $A$ in both cases, and its yield is the word $a$ for a leaf and the concatenation of the yields of the left and right subtrees for a node. A tree is valid for the grammar when every leaf's rule $A \to a$ and every node's rule $A \to B\,C$ is a rule of the grammar and, at every node, the left subtree has root $B$ and the right subtree has root $C$. For a nonterminal $A$ and a word $w$, the leaf trees are the leaf for $A \to a$ when $w$ is the single terminal $a$ and $A \to a$ is a rule, and nothing otherwise; the branch trees at depth bound $d$ are trees whose root is a node. For every $d$, $A$ and $w$, the two sets are disjoint.
