# Splits at different positions give disjoint trees

For a context-free grammar with nonterminals $N$ and terminals $\Sigma$, a derivation tree is either a leaf labelled by a nonterminal $A$ and a terminal $a$ (standing for the rule $A \to a$), or a node labelled by nonterminals $A, B, C$ with a left and a right subtree (standing for the rule $A \to B\,C$). The root of a tree is $A$ in both cases, and its yield is the word $a$ for a leaf and the concatenation of the yields of the left and right subtrees for a node. A tree is valid for the grammar when every leaf's rule $A \to a$ and every node's rule $A \to B\,C$ is a rule of the grammar and, at every node, the left subtree has root $B$ and the right subtree has root $C$. The depth of a leaf is $1$, and the depth of a node is one more than the larger depth of its subtrees. For a depth bound $d$, a nonterminal $A$ and a word $w$, the bounded tree set is the finite set of valid trees of depth at most $d$ with root $A$ and yield $w$. For nonterminals $A, B, C$, a depth bound $d$, a word $w$ and a split point $k \le |w|$, the trees at split $k$ are the nodes for $A \to B\,C$ whose left subtree lies in the bounded tree set of $B$ on $w_{<k}$ and whose right subtree lies in that of $C$ on $w_{\ge k}$, or none if the prefix or the suffix is empty. For two different split points $k_1 \ne k_2$, both at most $|w|$, the two sets of trees are disjoint.
