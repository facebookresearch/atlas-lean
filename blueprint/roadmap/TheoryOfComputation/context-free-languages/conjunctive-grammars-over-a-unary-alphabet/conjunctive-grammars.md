# Conjunctive grammars

A conjunctive grammar is a context-free grammar whose rules may conjoin several bodies: a string is generated by a rule exactly when it is generated by every conjunct.

A *conjunctive grammar* is a quadruple $G = (\Sigma, N, P, S)$ in which $\Sigma$ and $N$ are disjoint finite nonempty sets of terminal and nonterminal symbols, $S \in N$ is the start symbol and $P$ is a finite set of rules of the form

$$
A \to \alpha_1 \,\&\, \cdots \,\&\, \alpha_n \qquad (A \in N,\ n \ge 1,\ \alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n \in (\Sigma \cup N)^*).
$$

Derivations rewrite terms built from symbols of $\Sigma \cup N$ by concatenation and conjunction: a subterm $A$ may be replaced by $(\alpha_1 \& \cdots \& \alpha_n)$ for a rule of $A$, and a conjunction $(w \& \cdots \& w)$ of identical terminal strings may be replaced by $w$. The language of a term $\varphi$ is $L_G(\varphi) = \{w \in \Sigma^* : \varphi \Rightarrow^* w\}$, and $L(G) = L_G(S)$. Equivalently, the languages $L_G(A)$, $A \in N$, form the least solution of the system of language equations $A = \bigcup_{A \to \alpha_1 \& \cdots \& \alpha_m \in P} \bigcap_{i=1}^m \alpha_i$. A context-free grammar is the case where every rule has $n = 1$. A grammar is *unary* if $\Sigma = \{a\}$; its language is then identified with a set of natural numbers.

Context-free languages over a one-letter alphabet are regular, but unary conjunctive grammars generate non-regular languages, such as $\{a^{4^n} : n \ge 0\}$.
