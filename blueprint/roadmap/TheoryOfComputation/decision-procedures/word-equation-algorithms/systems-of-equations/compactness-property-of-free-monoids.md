# Compactness property of free monoids

Every system of constant-free word equations over finitely many unknowns, even an infinite one, has the same solutions in a free monoid as one of its finite subsystems. This is Ehrenfeucht's conjecture, proved by Albert and Lawrence and by Guba.

Let $\Xi$ be a finite set of unknowns and $\Sigma^*$ the free monoid over any alphabet $\Sigma$. For every set $T$ of constant-free word equations over $\Xi$ there is a finite subset $T_0 \subseteq T$ such that a morphism $h : \Xi^* \to \Sigma^*$ solves every equation of $T$ if and only if it solves every equation of $T_0$. Equivalently, every [decreasing chain](independent-systems-of-word-equations.md) of equations over $\Xi$ is finite. As a consequence, every independent system over $n$ unknowns is finite, that is, $\mathrm{IS}(n) \le \mathrm{ub}$.

The theorem gives no bound in terms of $n$ on the size of an independent system. Whether independent systems over three unknowns have bounded size is open: the largest known ones have three equations, and for systems with a nonperiodic solution only $2 \le \mathrm{IS}'(3) \le \mathrm{ub}$ is known.

## Expansion

The published proofs translate word equations into polynomial equations and apply Hilbert's basis theorem: M. H. Albert and J. Lawrence (Theoretical Computer Science 41 (1985)) embed the free monoid into a metabelian group, and V. S. Guba (Mathematical Notes 40 (1986)) uses a representation by matrices, which also gives the result for free groups. The following reconstruction of the matrix route is consistent with these descriptions but is not taken verbatim from a cited source. If $\Sigma$ is empty, the only morphism sends every unknown to $\varepsilon$ and solves every constant-free equation, so $T_0 = \emptyset$ works. Otherwise reduce to two letters: a morphism $h$ uses only finitely many letters $c_1, \dots, c_p$, and the injective morphism $g$ with $c_i \mapsto a^i b$ into $\{a, b\}^*$ satisfies $g(h(u)) = g(h(v))$ exactly when $h(u) = h(v)$; this covers in particular a one-letter alphabet. Over $\{a, b\}$, replace each unknown by a matrix of four integer unknowns and write each equation as four polynomial equations over $\mathbb{Z}$ in $4|\Xi|$ variables; since the two elementary unimodular matrices generate a free monoid, a morphism into $\{a, b\}^*$ solves an equation exactly when the associated point satisfies its polynomial equations. The ring $\mathbb{Z}[x_1, \dots, x_{4|\Xi|}]$ is Noetherian, by Hilbert's basis theorem applied $4|\Xi|$ times starting from the principal ideal domain $\mathbb{Z}$. So the ideal generated by the polynomials of all equations of $T$ is finitely generated; each of its finitely many generators is a combination of polynomials of finitely many equations of $T$, and these finitely many equations form $T_0$. Every point satisfying the polynomials of $T_0$ satisfies those of $T$, and for $g \circ h$ this means that $h$ solves $T$ whenever it solves $T_0$.

The equivalence with finiteness of decreasing chains is Theorem 3.2 of Karhumäki and Saarela: an infinite decreasing chain contradicts the existence of an equivalent finite subsystem, and conversely the indices at which the solution set of an infinite system strictly decreases form a decreasing chain. Every independent system is a decreasing chain in any order, which gives the consequence.
