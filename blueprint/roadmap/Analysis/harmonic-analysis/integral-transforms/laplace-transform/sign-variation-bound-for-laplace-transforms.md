# Sign-variation bound for Laplace transforms

Let $f : \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0} \to \mathbb{R}$ be piecewise continuous (continuous except at finitely many points of each bounded interval) and right-continuous, $f(t) = \lim_{u \downarrow t} f(u)$ for every $t \ge 0$, and not identically zero. Right-continuity excludes isolated point values, which the Laplace transform ignores: two right-continuous functions that agree almost everywhere agree everywhere, so the sign count below is determined by $f$ up to null sets, and a right-continuous $f$ that is not identically zero is nonzero on a set of positive measure. Let $\sigma(f)$ be the number of sign changes of $f$, counted by alternations of nonzero signs at increasing sample points with intervening zeros ignored: the supremum of the $k \ge 0$ for which there are $0 \le t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_k$ with $f(t_{i-1}) f(t_i) < 0$ for $1 \le i \le k$. Assume that $\sigma(f)$ is finite and that $f$ is of exponential order: $|f(t)| \le Me^{ct}$ for some constants $M$, $c$ and all $t \ge 0$. Then the Laplace transform $G(x) = \int_0^\infty f(t)e^{-tx}\,dt$, whose integral [converges absolutely](laplace-convergent-of-norm-le-exp.md) for every $x > c$, has at most $\sigma(f)$ zeros in $(c, \infty)$, counted with multiplicity.

This is a variant of the generalized Descartes rule of signs of Bhaskar and Song (2014, Theorem 4), and is not attributed to them. Their theorem is stated differently: they take a piecewise-continuous function that is not identically zero, without requiring right-continuity or exponential order, count its sign changes by their Definition 3 (a sign change point $t$ has $f = 0$ on some $(t, t')$, $t' \ge t$, and opposite strict signs on $(t - \varepsilon, t)$ and $(t', t' + \varepsilon)$, so intervals on which $f$ vanishes are skipped), and bound the number of roots of $G$ in $\mathbb{R}$, counted with multiplicity. The two counts disagree on functions whose zeros accumulate at the end of a gap of zeros (Expansion item 2), and agree when the zero set of $f$ in each bounded interval is a finite union of points and intervals, as for differences of piecewise-analytic functions.

## Expansion

1. The bound, by induction on $\sigma(f)$. If $\sigma(f) = 0$, then $sf \ge 0$ for a sign $s$ and, by right-continuity, $sf > 0$ on a nonempty interval, so $sG > 0$ on $(c, \infty)$. If $\sigma(f) = k \ge 1$, let $s$ be the sign for which $\{sf > 0\}$ has the smaller infimum (the two infima differ, since otherwise $f$ would alternate infinitely often near their common value) and $a = \inf\{t : sf(t) < 0\}$, so $sf \ge 0$ on $[0, a)$, $sf(v) > 0$ for some $v < a$, and $sf$ takes negative values in every interval $(a, a + \delta)$. Then $\frac{d}{dx}\bigl(e^{ax}G(x)\bigr) = -\int_0^\infty (t - a) f(t) e^{-x(t - a)}\, dt$, and $g(t) = (t - a) f(t)$ is right-continuous, piecewise continuous, not identically zero, of exponential order $c'$ for every $c' > c$, and has $\sigma(g) \le k - 1$: an alternating sequence for $g$ extends, by a point $v < a$ with $sf(v) > 0$ and, if needed, a point just right of $a$ where $sf < 0$, to one for $f$ with at least one more alternation. By induction and Rolle's theorem, counting multiplicities, $e^{ax}G(x)$, and hence $G$, has at most $k$ zeros in $(c, \infty)$.
2. The example showing why Definition 3 alone does not suffice: $f = 1$ on $[0, 1)$, $f(t) = -A(t - 3)\sin^2(\pi/(t - 3))$ on $(3, 4)$ and $f = 0$ elsewhere, with $A > 0$ chosen so that $G(1) = 0$; Definition 3 finds no sign change point, while the alternation count is $1$.
