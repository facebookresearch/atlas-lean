# Sign-variation bound for Laplace transforms

Let $f : \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0} \to \mathbb{R}$ be piecewise continuous (continuous except at finitely many points of each bounded interval) and right-continuous, $f(t) = \lim_{u \downarrow t} f(u)$ for every $t \ge 0$, and not identically zero. Right-continuity excludes isolated point values, which the Laplace transform ignores: two right-continuous functions that agree almost everywhere agree everywhere, so the sign count below is determined by $f$ up to null sets, and a right-continuous $f$ that is not identically zero is nonzero on a set of positive measure. Let $\sigma(f)$ be the number of sign changes of $f$, counted by alternations of nonzero signs at increasing sample points with intervening zeros ignored: the supremum of the $k \ge 0$ for which there are $0 \le t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_k$ with $f(t_{i-1}) f(t_i) < 0$ for $1 \le i \le k$. Assume that $\sigma(f)$ is finite and that $f$ is of exponential order: $|f(t)| \le Me^{ct}$ for some constants $M$, $c$ and all $t \ge 0$. Then the Laplace transform $G(x) = \int_0^\infty f(t)e^{-tx}\,dt$, whose integral [converges absolutely](laplace-convergent-of-norm-le-exp.md) for every $x > c$, has at most $\sigma(f)$ zeros in $(c, \infty)$, counted with multiplicity.

This is a variant of the generalized Descartes rule of signs of Bhaskar and Song (2014, Theorem 4), and is not attributed to them. Their theorem is stated differently: they take a piecewise-continuous function that is not identically zero, without requiring right-continuity or exponential order, count its sign changes by their Definition 3 (a sign change point $t$ has $f = 0$ on some $(t, t')$, $t' \ge t$, and opposite strict signs on $(t - \varepsilon, t)$ and $(t', t' + \varepsilon)$, so intervals on which $f$ vanishes are skipped), and bound the number of roots of $G$ in $\mathbb{R}$, counted with multiplicity. The two counts disagree on functions whose zeros accumulate at the end of a gap of zeros (Expansion item 2), and agree when the zero set of $f$ in each bounded interval is a finite union of points and intervals, for example when $f$ is piecewise analytic with pieces that extend analytically across their finite endpoints; analyticity on the open pieces alone does not suffice, since zeros may accumulate at an endpoint, as in Expansion item 2.

## Expansion

1. The bound, by induction on $k = \sigma(f)$. For $x > c$ the Laplace transform is [holomorphic](differentiable-on-laplace-of-norm-le-exp.md) and $G'(x) = -\int_0^\infty t f(t) e^{-xt}\,dt$, differentiation under the integral sign being justified by dominated convergence: for $x \ge x_0 > c$, $|t f(t) e^{-xt}| \le M t e^{-(x_0 - c)t}$, which is integrable; absolute convergence of the integral alone would not suffice. $G$ is real-analytic and not identically zero on $(c, \infty)$, so its zeros there have finite multiplicities. If $k = 0$, then $sf \ge 0$ for a sign $s$ and, by right-continuity, $sf > 0$ on a nonempty interval, so $sG > 0$ on $(c, \infty)$. If $k \ge 1$, let $s$ be the sign for which $\{sf > 0\}$ has the smaller infimum (the two infima differ, since otherwise $f$ would alternate infinitely often near their common value) and $a = \inf\{t : sf(t) < 0\}$, so $sf \ge 0$ on $[0, a)$, $sf(v) > 0$ for some $v < a$, and $sf$ takes negative values in every interval $(a, a + \delta)$. The function $g(t) = (t - a) f(t)$ is right-continuous, piecewise continuous and not identically zero, and $\sigma(g) \le k - 1$: an alternating sequence for $g$ extends, by a point $v < a$ with $sf(v) > 0$ and, if needed, a point just right of $a$ where $sf < 0$, to one for $f$ with at least one more alternation. Let $Z \subset (c, \infty)$ be any finite set of zeros of $G$, of total multiplicity $m$, and choose $c'$ with $c < c' < \min Z$; then $|g(t)| \le M' e^{c' t}$ for some $M'$, so $g$ has exponential order $c'$. With $h(x) = e^{ax} G(x)$ we have $h'(x) = -e^{ax} \int_0^\infty g(t) e^{-xt}\,dt$, and by the induction hypothesis the Laplace transform of $g$ has at most $k - 1$ zeros in $(c', \infty)$, counted with multiplicity. The zeros of $h$ in $Z$ have the multiplicities of those of $G$, so Rolle's theorem with multiplicities gives at least $m - 1$ zeros of $h'$, counted with multiplicity, in $[\min Z, \max Z] \subset (c', \infty)$. Hence $m - 1 \le k - 1$, so $m \le k$; since $Z$ was arbitrary, $G$ has at most $k$ zeros in $(c, \infty)$.
2. The example showing why Definition 3 alone does not suffice: $f = 1$ on $[0, 1)$, $f(t) = -A(t - 3)\sin^2(\pi/(t - 3))$ on $(3, 4)$ and $f = 0$ elsewhere, with $A > 0$ chosen so that $G(1) = 0$; Definition 3 finds no sign change point, while the alternation count is $1$.
