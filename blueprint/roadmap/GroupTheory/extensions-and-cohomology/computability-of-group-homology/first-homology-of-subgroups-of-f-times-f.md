# The first homology of finitely generated subgroups of a direct product of two free groups is not computable

For non-abelian free groups $F_1$ and $F_2$, no algorithm computes, for a finitely generated subgroup $G \leq F_1 \times F_2$ given by generators, the torsion-free rank of $H_1(G, \mathbb{Z})$ or decides whether $H_1(G, \mathbb{Z})$ has non-zero torsion.

Let $F_1$ and $F_2$ be non-abelian free groups of finite rank. An instance is a finite set of elements of $F_1 \times F_2$, each given as a pair of words, generating a subgroup $G$; $H_1(G, \mathbb{Z}) = G / [G, G]$ is its first integral homology, its abelianization.

**Theorem (Bridson and Miller).** There is a recursive collection of finitely generated subgroups $G_i$ of $F_1 \times F_2$ such that there is no algorithm to compute the rank of $H_1(G_i, \mathbb{Z})$ or to determine whether it has any non-trivial torsion elements (Corollary 3.4 of arXiv:0708.4331v1). Hence there is no algorithm to compute, for an arbitrary finitely generated subgroup $G \leq F_1 \times F_2$, the torsion-free rank of $H_1(G, \mathbb{Z})$, nor to determine whether $H_1(G, \mathbb{Z})$ has non-zero torsion elements.

The same paper recalls, from Baumslag and Roseblade, that there are continuously many subdirect products $G \leq F_1 \times F_2$ with pairwise non-isomorphic $H_1(G, \mathbb{Z})$.

## Expansion

For a subdirect product $G \leq F_1 \times F_2$ with $L_i = G \cap F_i$, the source proves $H_1(G, \mathbb{Z}) \cong H_1(F_2, \mathbb{Z}) \oplus H_2(F_1 / L_1, \mathbb{Z}) \oplus C$ with $C$ free abelian of rank at most the rank of $F_1$ (Corollary 3.2). Gordon's theorem gives a recursive family of finite presentations $F \to \Gamma_i$ on a common generating set, each $\Gamma_i$ perfect and either trivial or with $H_2(\Gamma_i, \mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, for which no algorithm decides which case holds. The fiber product $G_i = \{(u, v) \in F \times F : u, v \text{ have the same image in } \Gamma_i\}$ is generated by the diagonal and the relators in one factor, so it is finitely generated and computable from the presentation, and since $\Gamma_i$ is perfect, $H_1(G_i, \mathbb{Z}) \cong H_1(F, \mathbb{Z}) \oplus H_2(\Gamma_i, \mathbb{Z}) \oplus H_1(F, \mathbb{Z})$. Computing the rank or the torsion of $H_1(G_i, \mathbb{Z})$ would decide the case. A complete expansion links a page for Gordon's theorem that no algorithm decides whether $H_2$ of a finitely presented group vanishes, which has none.
