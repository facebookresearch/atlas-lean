# Lower bound for nonadaptive semi-quantitative group testing

**Setting.** A population of $n$ individuals is screened by pooled qPCR tests. Each individual is infected independently with a constant probability $p\in(0,1)$. In **semi-quantitative group testing** (SQGT) with saturation level $m\ge1$, a test applied to a pool returns $i$ if the pool contains exactly $i$ infected individuals for $i<m$, and returns "$\ge m$" otherwise; $m=1$ is classical binary group testing, and larger $m$ models the extra information in qPCR amplification curves (cycle-threshold values) up to their saturation limit. A *nonadaptive* scheme fixes all pools in advance, and it must recover every infection status with error probability at most $\varepsilon$.

**Theorem 1.** For every $m\ge1$ and every constant $p\in(0,1)$ there is a constant $\varepsilon(m,p)>0$ such that every nonadaptive SQGT scheme with error probability below $\varepsilon(m,p)$ uses at least $n/m$ tests.

## Expansion

Discard every test with at most $m$ members together with the individuals in it; this removes at most $m$ individuals per test, so it suffices to show that a scheme in which every test has at least $m+1$ members cannot have arbitrarily small error. Call individual $i$ *disguised* if each test containing $i$ also contains at least $m$ other infected individuals. Flipping the status of a disguised individual changes no test outcome, so the decoder can only guess it and errs with probability at least $\min(p,1-p)$. The events "disguised in test $t$" are increasing in the set of infected individuals, so by the FKG inequality $\Pr[i\text{ disguised}]\ge\prod_{t\ni i}\Pr[\mathrm{Bin}(w_t-1,p)\ge m]$, where $w_t\ge m+1$ is the size of test $t$; averaging the logarithm of this bound over individuals shows that some individual is disguised with probability bounded below by a constant depending only on $m$ and $p$. The case $m=1$ is the known result that individual testing is optimal for nonadaptive probabilistic group testing at constant prevalence. In contrast, the paper's two-stage adaptive SQGT protocol uses far fewer than $n/2$ tests for $m=2$ at moderate prevalence, and its case study accounts for dilution and the load-dependent false negative rates of PCR.
