# Allele age under changing population size

With a changing population size, the age of an allele observed today at frequency $x$ is defined with mutations arising in calendar time at a rate proportional to the population size at the time and with the time-inhomogeneous transition laws of the diffusion. If $\nu(t)$ is the relative size at time $t$, measured back from the present at $t = 0$, new mutants arise at rate proportional to $\nu(t)$ at frequency $1/(M\nu(t))$, and $q_{t}(\cdot, x)$ is the density of the killed time-inhomogeneous diffusion from time $-t$ to time $0$, then the density of the age $t$ of an allele observed at frequency $x$ is proportional to $\nu(t)\,q_t(1/(M\nu(t)), x)$, normalized over $t > 0$; for constant size this reduces to the usual age density built from the stationary killed transition density. The constant-size results, such as the neutral mean age and the symmetry of the mean age under a change of sign of genic selection, are statements about constant size and do not transfer to changing size without a separate argument. Kiezun et al. (2013) test the prediction of Maruyama (1974) that a slightly deleterious allele is on average younger than a neutral allele at the same frequency, and find in forward-in-time simulations that the difference in mean age is present in a recently rapidly expanding population and in a bottleneck followed by rapid expansion, though smaller than in a constant-size population (their Figure 2).
