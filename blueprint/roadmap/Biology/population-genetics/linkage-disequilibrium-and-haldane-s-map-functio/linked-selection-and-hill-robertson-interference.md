# Linked selection with recombination: background effects on fixation

Barton (1995, abstract) gives a general method for the fixation probability of a favourable allele that can find itself in a variety of genetic backgrounds, applied to a large population in which selection at other loci causes inherited variation in fitness, and finds the following effects of linked selection with recombination. With loose linkage $r$, the effects depend on the additive genetic variance in relative fitness, $\mathrm{var}(W)$, and act by reducing the effective population size by $N/N_e = 1 + \mathrm{var}(W)/2r^2$. Linked deleterious mutations reduce the fixation probability of weakly favoured alleles by the factor $\exp(-2U/R)$, where $U$ is the total mutation rate and $R$ the map length in Morgans. Substitutions at linked loci can cause a greater reduction: an allele with advantage $s$ below $s_{crit} = (\pi^2/6)\log_e(S/s)\,\mathrm{var}(W)/R$, where $S$ is the advantage of the substitution impeding fixation, is very unlikely to be fixed. Tightly linked loci can have a substantial effect not predictable from $N_e$. This is the quantitative, positive-recombination side of the Hill–Robertson effect, in which linkage disequilibria generated in a finite population slow the response to selection and recombination speeds it (Hill–Robertson effect, Wikipedia).

## Expansion

1. The fixation probability of a favourable allele averaged over genetic backgrounds that vary in fitness.
2. The loose-linkage reduction of effective size, $N/N_e = 1 + \mathrm{var}(W)/2r^2$.
3. Background selection by linked deleterious mutations and the factor $\exp(-2U/R)$.
4. Interference from linked substitutions and the critical advantage $s_{crit}$.
5. Fitness schemes with epistasis in which interference changes sign, as separate statements with their own sources.
