# Kinetic pairing and the RPA polynomial of reaction networks

**Setting.** A chemical reaction network with species $x_1, \dots, x_n$ under mass-action kinetics has polynomial rate equations $\dot x = f(x)$, $f = (f_1, \dots, f_n) \in \mathbb R[x_1, \dots, x_n]^n$ (rate constants as parameters, with possibly a constant input $I$ entering some rates). The network exhibits *robust perfect adaptation* (RPA) in species $x_i$ with setpoint $c$ if at every steady state reachable under any persistent constant disturbance $x_i = c$, with $c$ independent of the disturbance and of initial conditions, and the steady state is stable.

**Theorem (two-variable kinetic pairing).** Here a *variable* is a species, or a power product of species that occur in the rate equations only through that product (a boundary variable), and $X_k^{\mathrm{tot}}$ is the largest possible concentration of $x_k$. The network has the capacity for RPA in the variable $x_i$ if and only if, for every variable $x_j$ that does not exhibit RPA, the elimination ideal $\langle f_1, \dots, f_n\rangle \cap \mathbb R[x_i, x_j]$ is a principal ideal generated by a polynomial of the form

$$\rho = g(x_i, x_j)\,(x_i - c), \qquad \rho = h_1 f_1 + \cdots + h_n f_n \ \text{ for some } h_1, \dots, h_n \in \mathbb R[x_1, \dots, x_n],$$

where $g \in \mathbb R[x_i, x_j]$ is non-vanishing on $(0, X_i^{\mathrm{tot}}) \times (0, X_j^{\mathrm{tot}})$ and the setpoint $c$ is a rational function of the system parameters. The generator $\rho$, in lowest-order form, is the *RPA polynomial*: a polynomial in exactly two variables lying in the ideal generated by the rate equations. Since $g \ne 0$ on the box, it forces $x_i = c$ at every positive steady state.

**Topological interpretation.** The two cases are distinguished by the direction of regulation between $x_i$ and $x_j$; in both, $\partial\rho/\partial x_j$ vanishes at steady state. If $x_i$ regulates $x_j$ (directly or indirectly), the upregulating and downregulating contributions of $x_j$ to its own rate are paired through $g$; the steady-state condition $\partial\rho/\partial x_j = 0$ is then opposer kinetics, $x_j$ is an opposer variable, and the network has the feedback structure of an *Opposer module*. If $x_j$ regulates $x_i$, the contributions of $x_j$ to the rate of $x_i$ are paired through $g$; the same condition is then connector kinetics (subject to balancer kinetics on the reactions inside the parallel pathways), $x_j$ is a diverter variable, $x_i$ a connector, and the network has the incoherent feedforward structure of a *Balancer module*. The polynomial combination can always be decomposed into linear subproblems over algebraically independent subnetworks, each producing an opposer, balancer or connector invariant obtainable by at most a linear change of coordinates; these are joined by "concatenating monomials". The number of independent invariants a subnetwork can supply is controlled by its deficiency.

**Example (antithetic integral control).** With species $R$ (output), $X_1$, $X_2$ and reactions $R \xrightarrow{k_1} R + X_1$, $\varnothing \xrightarrow{k_3} X_2$, $X_1 + X_2 \xrightarrow{k_2} \varnothing$ (plus arbitrary regulation of $R$ by $X_1$), the combination $\dot X_1 - \dot X_2 = k_1 R - k_3$ is a linear invariant: the integral variable $z = X_1 - X_2 = \int (k_1 R - k_3)\, dt$ forces $R = k_3 / k_1$ at any steady state.

## Expansion

The internal model principle says an RPA-capable system can be transformed so that one coordinate integrates the output error, $\dot z = x_i - c$ (up to a nonvanishing factor). For mass-action networks the coordinate change need not be global or linear, but the integrand must be generated by the rate equations, which places $g(x_i, x_j)(x_i - c)$ in the ideal $\langle f_1, \dots, f_n \rangle$. That only two variables appear is the content of kinetic pairing: the integrator couples the adapting species with exactly one non-adapting partner. This yields an algorithmic test (Gröbner-basis elimination in the ideal) that applies at any deficiency, unlike the Shinar–Feinberg theorem for absolute concentration robustness, which needs deficiency one. In the EnvZ–OmpR osmoregulation network (deficiency two), a balancer invariant fixes the ratio of EnvZ–ATP to EnvZ–ADP, and a connector invariant then fixes phosphorylated OmpR.
