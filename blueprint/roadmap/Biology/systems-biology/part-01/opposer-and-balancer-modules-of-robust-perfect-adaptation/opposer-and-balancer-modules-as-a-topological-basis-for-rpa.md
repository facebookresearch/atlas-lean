# Opposer and Balancer Modules as a topological basis for RPA

**Setting.** Consider a network with input node, output node, steady-state Jacobian $J_n$ and RPA equation $\det(M_{IO}) = 0$ as in [RPA equation for an input–output node pair](rpa-equation-for-an-input-output-node-pair.md). Let $R$ be the set of signed terms of the determinant expansion of $\det(M_{IO})$, each a product of a route factor, circuit products and kinetic multipliers. A subset of $R$ is *independently adapting* if its terms sum to zero for all inputs $I$ on their own, and *minimally adaptive* if it has no proper independently adapting subset. Minimally adaptive subsets are of two kinds:

- **S-sets** (singletons), whose term vanishes for all $I$;
- **M-sets** (multi-term), whose terms are all nonzero but cancel for all $I$.

**Opposition (S-sets).** An S-set arises when an *opposer node* $P_o$, appearing as a kinetic multiplier, has *opposer kinetics*

$$\frac{\partial f_o}{\partial P_o} = 0 \quad \text{at steady state, for all } I .$$

Then: (i) a node can have opposer kinetics only if it lies on a feedback loop, and it needs a single independent regulator sharing a circuit with it; (ii) a single opposer opposes every term of a given route iff it is disjoint from the route and participates only in circuits contiguous with the route; (iii) collections of opposers distributed one per interlinked circuit, embedded in a feedback loop contiguous with the route, form *opposing sets*. The resulting topologies are **Opposer Modules**. Each opposer computes the integral of a tracking error of its regulator; the regulator (and its dependents) has RPA, the opposer never does. Antithetic integral feedback is a two-node opposing set.

**Balancing (M-sets).** Every M-set contains at least two distinct input–output routes; hence a network whose input node is also its output node cannot use balancing and must rely on opposition. Balancing constrains a *diverter node* $D$ at the apex, *balancer nodes* on the route segments between $D$ and a *connector node* $C$ at the base, whose steady states lie on a flat manifold (each balancer's steady state is a linear function of $D$'s), and $C$ completes the cancellation. The resulting topologies are **Balancer Modules**; only the connector node has RPA.

**Basis theorem.** A network has RPA only if $R$ partitions into disjoint independently adapting subsets, each generated by opposition or by balancing. Equivalently, every input–output route must be fully opposed by one Opposer Module or balanced by one Balancer Module. Opposer, balancer and connector kinetics are mutually exclusive at a node, so module active parts are distinct and modules interconnect only *in parallel* or *in series*. Outgoing links from RPA-exhibiting nodes ("blind" regulations) impose no further requirement; links from non-RPA nodes such as opposers or balancers ("live" regulations) create routes that a downstream module must oppose or balance.

## Expansion

Each term of $\det(M_{IO})$ is $\pm$ (route factor) $\times$ (product of disjoint circuits) $\times$ (kinetic multipliers $\partial f_k/\partial P_k$ of the remaining nodes). For a term to vanish identically in $I$ without killing the route itself, some factor in its cycle part must vanish identically; a kinetic multiplier of a node off the route can vanish structurally only through zero-order (saturated) kinetics, which is opposer kinetics, and that node must lie on a circuit that touches the route in order to sit in the same terms. Terms that cannot be killed must cancel against others with the same cycle structure but different routes, so at least two routes are needed, and the cancellation for all $I$ forces linear steady-state relations along the route segments (the flat manifold) closed off by the connector. Since all instances of a route are absorbed by a single mechanism, the independently adapting subsets are disjoint and cover $R$, which gives the modular decomposition.
