# Yield-optimal solutions from elementary flux vectors

Keep the notation of [yield optimization as an LP](yield-optimization-as-a-linear-program-via-charnes-cooper.md): $Y(x) = c^{\mathsf T}x / d^{\mathsf T}x$ on $P_{>} = \{x \in P : d^{\mathsf T}x > 0\}$. Let $P_{\ge} = \{ x \in P : d^{\mathsf T} x \ge 0 \}$, the smallest polyhedron containing $P_{>}$, and by Minkowski's theorem write every $x \in P_{\ge}$ as

$$x = \sum_{i \in I} \alpha_i v_i + \sum_{j \in J} \beta_j u_j, \qquad \alpha_i, \beta_j \ge 0,\ \sum_{i \in I} \alpha_i = 1,$$

with $v_i$ generators of the polytope part and $u_j$ generators of the recession cone (for instance elementary flux vectors). A vector $x \in P_{\ge}$ with $d^{\mathsf T} x = 0$, or a recession generator $u_j$ with $d^{\mathsf T}u_j = 0$, has **unbounded yield** if $c^{\mathsf T}x \ne 0$ (respectively $c^{\mathsf T}u_j \ne 0$) and **undefined yield** if it is $0$. Put $I_d = \{ i : d^{\mathsf T}v_i > 0 \}$ and $J_d = \{ j : d^{\mathsf T}u_j > 0 \}$; recession generators are classified by the signs of $d^{\mathsf T}u_j$ and $c^{\mathsf T}u_j$, not by membership in $P$, which they need not have (for instance under a positive lower flux bound). The source defines $J_d = \{j : u_j \in P_>\}$, which agrees when $0 \in P$.

**Facts.** (a) For $x, y \in D$ and $\lambda \in [0,1]$, $Y((1-\lambda)x + \lambda y) = (1-\lambda')Y(x) + \lambda' Y(y)$ with $\lambda' = \lambda\, d^{\mathsf T}y / \big((1-\lambda)d^{\mathsf T}x + \lambda d^{\mathsf T}y\big)$, and $\lambda \mapsto \lambda'$ is an increasing bijection of $[0,1]$. (b) If some point of $P_{\ge}$ has unbounded yield, $Y$ is unbounded on $P_{>}$. (c) Adding a vector of undefined yield does not change the yield. (d) If $Y$ is bounded on $P_{>}$, the yield of any $x \in P_{>}$ is a convex combination of the yields $Y(v_i)$, $i \in I_d$, and $Y(u_j)$, $j \in J_d$, with positive weight exactly on the generators that appear in $x$.

**Theorem 1 (attainment).** Let $Y$ be bounded on the nonempty set $P_{>}$. The maximum yield is **not** attained iff $I_d = I$ and $\max_{i \in I} Y(v_i) < \max_{j \in J_d} Y(u_j)$. In that case the supremum $Y^*$ is approached along $v + \beta u_j$ with $Y(u_j) = Y^*$ and $\beta \to \infty$.

**Theorem 2 (optimal set).** Let $Y$ be bounded on $P_{>}$ with attained maximum $Y^*$. Then $x^* \in P_{>}$ is yield-optimal iff

$$x^* = \sum_{i \in I^*} \alpha_i^* v_i + \sum_{i \in I_u} \alpha_i v_i + \sum_{j \in J^*} \beta_j^* u_j + \sum_{j \in J_u} \beta_j u_j,$$

where $I^*, J^*$ index generators with $Y = Y^*$, $I_u, J_u$ index generators of undefined yield, all coefficients are nonnegative, $\sum_{I^*} \alpha_i^* + \sum_{I_u} \alpha_i = 1$, and $\sum_{I^*} \alpha_i^* + \sum_{J^*} \beta_j^* > 0$. Hence the closure of the yield-optimal set is a polyhedron generated by the optimal-yield and undefined-yield generators.

**Special cases.** Assume $d^{\mathsf T}x \ge 0$ on $P$ (so $P = P_{\ge}$) and $Y$ bounded on the nonempty $P_{>}$. On the flux cone ($P$ given by steady state and irreversibility only) the yield-optimal solutions are exactly the conical sums of elementary flux modes of maximal or undefined yield, containing at least one of maximal yield. On a bounded flux polytope they are the convex sums of bounded elementary flux vectors of maximal or undefined yield. For several yields with a common denominator, the **yield space** $\{ (Y_1(x), \dots, Y_\ell(x)) : x \in P_{>} \}$ is convex, and when all yields are bounded and attained it equals the polytope spanned by the yield vectors of the generators with defined yield.

## Expansion

Fact (a) is a direct computation: $Y(z) = \big((1-\lambda)c^{\mathsf T}x + \lambda c^{\mathsf T}y\big)/\big((1-\lambda)d^{\mathsf T}x + \lambda d^{\mathsf T}y\big)$, with $d\lambda'/d\lambda = d^{\mathsf T}x\, d^{\mathsf T}y / ((1-\lambda)d^{\mathsf T}x + \lambda d^{\mathsf T}y)^2 > 0$. Iterating (a) over the generator decomposition, after discarding undefined-yield terms by (c), gives (d). For Theorem 1: if some $v_i$ has undefined yield, it can be combined with an optimal ray at finite scale, and if some $v_i$ attains the largest generator yield, the maximum is attained there; otherwise every finite point mixes in a strictly worse bounded generator with positive weight, so the supremum is reached only as the ray coefficient diverges. Theorem 2 follows from (d): a convex combination equals its maximum only when all positive-weight terms have the maximal value.
