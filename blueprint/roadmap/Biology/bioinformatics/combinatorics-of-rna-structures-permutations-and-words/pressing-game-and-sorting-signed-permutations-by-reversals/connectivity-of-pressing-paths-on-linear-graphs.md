# Pressing game on black-and-white graphs and connectivity of pressing paths on linear graphs

**Sorting by reversals.** A reversal of a signed permutation of $\{1, \dots, n\}$ reverses a consecutive segment and flips the sign of each entry in it; the reversal distance $d_{REV}(\pi)$ is the minimum number of reversals transforming $\pi$ into $+1\ +2 \cdots +n$, a standard model of genome rearrangement. In the graph of desire and reality (each $+i$ written as $2i-1, 2i$ and each $-i$ as $2i, 2i-1$, framed by $0$ and $2n+1$), the desire edges join $2i$ and $2i+1$. The **overlap graph** has the desire edges as vertices, colors a vertex black (oriented) if its desire edge spans an odd number of positions and white otherwise, and joins two vertices whose spanned intervals overlap without containment. Hannenhalli and Pevzner showed $d_{REV}(\pi) = n + 1 - c(\pi) + h(\pi) + f(\pi)$, with $c$ the number of cycles of the graph of desire and reality, $h$ the number of hurdles and $f$ the fortress indicator. A reversal acting on an oriented desire edge $v$ toggles the orientation of every desire edge crossing $v$, toggles the overlap of every pair of desire edges crossing $v$, and leaves $v$ unoriented and isolated (Lemma 1).

**Pressing game.** On a graph $G$ with black and white vertices, pressing a black vertex $v$ (a) flips the color of each neighbor of $v$, (b) complements the adjacency among the neighbors of $v$, and (c) makes $v$ an isolated white vertex. A **successful pressing path** is a sequence of presses, each on a currently black vertex, ending in the all-white empty graph. This abstracts optimal sorting of permutations whose overlap graph has no non-trivial unoriented component, each press being a reversal on an oriented desire edge.

**Known facts.** If every component of $G$ contains a black vertex, then a black vertex can always be pressed without creating a non-trivial all-white component (Theorem 3), so $G$ can be reduced to the all-white empty graph (Theorem 4), and all successful pressing paths of $G$ have the same length (Theorem 5).

**Theorem 7 (pressing game conjecture for linear graphs).** Let $G$ be a finite black-and-white path (linear graph), and let $M$ be the graph whose vertices are the successful pressing paths of $G$, two paths being adjacent if their longest common subsequence has length at least their common length minus $2$. Then $M$ is connected.

So on linear graphs any optimal pressing path can be turned into any other by a sequence of moves that each delete at most two presses and insert at most two presses, which is stronger than the general conjecture (Conjecture 6), where up to four presses may change at each step.

## Expansion

Pressing a black vertex of a path joins its two neighbors and isolates it, so the result is again a path plus an isolated white vertex (self-reducibility). To connect two successful paths $X$ and $Y = v_1 v_2 \cdots v_k$, it suffices to transform $X$ by allowed moves into a successful path starting with $v_1$: then both suffixes are successful paths on the smaller path $G v_1 \setminus \{v_1\}$ and induction applies. Since $v_1$ is black in $G$, either $v_1$ occurs in $X$, or $X$ presses its unique black neighbor $u$ when only $u$ and $v_1$ remain, in which case pressing $v_1$ instead of $u$ is a one-letter change. A case analysis on the positions and colors of the neighbors of $v_1$ along the path shows that $v_1$ can be moved step by step toward the front of the pressing path by changes that alter at most two presses. These metagraph connectivity results are the irreducibility step needed for Markov chain Monte Carlo sampling of optimal reversal scenarios, used to quantify uncertainty in genome rearrangement histories.
