# A subset of a uniform space is compact if and only if it is complete and totally bounded

A metric space is compact if and only if it is complete and totally bounded, meaning that for every $\varepsilon > 0$ it is covered by finitely many balls of radius $\varepsilon$. Mathlib proves this more generally, for subsets of uniform spaces. Let $X$ be a uniform space and $s \subseteq X$. The set $s$ is *totally bounded* if for every entourage $V$ of $X$ there is a finite set $t \subseteq X$ such that every $x \in s$ satisfies $(x, y) \in V$ for some $y \in t$. The set $s$ is *complete* if every Cauchy filter on $X$ that contains $s$ converges to a point of $s$. Then $s$ is compact if and only if $s$ is totally bounded and complete. Completeness is required of $s$ itself, not of $X$, and no separation axiom is assumed. Total boundedness is stronger than boundedness: an infinite set with the metric $d(x, y) = 1$ for $x \ne y$ is bounded but not totally bounded. For a metric space $(X, d)$ with its uniform structure, whose basic entourages are the sets $\{(x, y) : d(x, y) < \varepsilon\}$, total boundedness of $X$ is the covering by finitely many $\varepsilon$-balls, and completeness of $X$ in the filter sense is equivalent to the convergence of every Cauchy sequence; taking $s = X$ gives the familiar statement.
